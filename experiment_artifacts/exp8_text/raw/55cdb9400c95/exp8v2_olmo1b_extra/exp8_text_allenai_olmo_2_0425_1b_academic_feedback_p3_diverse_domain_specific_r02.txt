The state of the art for AI in the field of computer vision is generally impressive, and few researchers are still looking for new ways of improving the quality of the results produced by the systems. One area in which research is currently being undertaken is the study of human motion. Since vision is the most promising channel for the acquisition of motion data, researchers have been working on developing more efficient algorithms that can be used to estimate human motion from camera images. In this body of work, the motion of people is generally described as a highly stochastic process, so previous models were designed to capture only relevant information from the input data. More recently, when the goal is to predict different aspects of the human motion behavior, such as the detection of a person's pose in a video, the output is usually described in terms of a single hypothesis, such as the existence or the activity of a person. Thus, the prediction process is reduced to an inference problem, where the goal is to identify the most likely hypothesis among a given set of possible hypotheses. The main advantage of these types of models is that they are easy to train and invert, and, therefore, they can be developed for large-scale systems. However, since they only produce a single hypothesis, this approach has a number of drawbacks. For example, the system may only be able to detect the presence or the absence of an object, or it may be able to detect only a small part of the object, making it difficult to detect its full shape. In addition, if the system is unable to detect any object at all, it may be unable to capture any motion at all.
