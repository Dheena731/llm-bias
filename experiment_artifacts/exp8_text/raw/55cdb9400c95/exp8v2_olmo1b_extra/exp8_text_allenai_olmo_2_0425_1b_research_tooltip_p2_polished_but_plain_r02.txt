The following are some additional notes that refer directly to this guide.

  1. To make a plot, have a clear title that explains what the plot is about. The title should be short and the information conveyed in the first two words. Examples: these plots show the effect of cigarette use on the risk of lung cancer, smoking and lung cancer mortality in Spain, and the impact of smoking on the risk of lung cancer in the UK and Spain.
  2. Use a legend to include information about the color and shape of the various symbols used in the plot. The first line of the legend should explain the abbreviations of the symbols. For example, lines, bars, lines, or diamonds.
  3. Use a simple and clear x-axis label and y-axis label. The label for the x-axis should be the variable that is on the horizontal axis, and the label for the y-axis should be the variable that is on the vertical axis. For example, variables: years, age, race, income, and occupation.
  4. Use descriptive names, not single letter codes, for the categories contained in the x and y axis. This is especially important in area plots. For example, use Spanish for Latin American countries.
  5. Use subplots (not separate plots) to show data that is similar in shape. They can also be used to show relationship between two variables. For example, use subplots to show the effect of education on income, and show the relationship between age and mortality.
  6. Draw horizontal lines on a scatterplot to show that the points are binned. For example, you can draw a horizontal line at the median of the x-axis. Another example is to draw a horizontal line at the 5th percentile of the x-axis. This is an important way to visualize the relationship between two variables, especially if you are trying to show outliers.
  7. Use a title for the legend, not the axis labels. For example, your legend should be titled "Country" or "Age" and NOT "Country" and "Age".

Formatting conventions

This wikibook uses these conventions for formatting text:

  • Use 2 spaces after a period at the end of a sentence. This is done so that text is more visually appealing when displayed separately from code or mathematical expressions.
  • Use \ to start a math expression or to create one that has a math type font. For example, \$x\$, and \$x + 2\$.
  • Use \[ to create a math expression enclosed in parentheses. For example, \(2x + 3\).
  • Use \displaystyle to get a text font with the math type font. For example, \[\frac{1}{-1}\cdot\frac{2x}{y}\] is a math expression that is displayed in a text font.
  • Use \textstyle to get a text font with a regular font. For example, \text{the }x\text{ belongs to the set }A\text{.}
  • Use \textup{ and \textdown to get a text font with a fixed-width font. For example, \textup{ the }x\text{ belongs to the set }A.\textup.
