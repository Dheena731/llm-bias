What Makes a Good Poster?

"Good" posters are not just pretty pictures. A poster must state its purpose clearly. Be sure the poster is divided into sections that are clearly labeled. A poster should be easy to read, have a good contrast between its text and background, and include a title. Check your poster for accuracy and make sure it is understandable. Make sure the poster does not contain any information that is not relevant to its purpose.


What Makes a Poor Poster?

Be sure the poster is not too wordy. Keep it short and sweet and concise. Avoid jargon. Read your poster over and make sure it is understandable. Use big print and a limited color palette. Don't make the poster too small. It is best to use 11 or 12 point font for posters. Be sure to include the author's name and affiliation (if it is not the college's) in the poster. If possible, send the poster in the format of an .eps file (posters can be printed from most word processors) to the poster display committee.


The Lighthouse Example (poster draft)

What is the topic of the poster? What is the purpose of the poster? What is the general question being asked? The general question is the title of the poster. Note that this poster is an abstract (it states the purpose of the poster without offering evidence). Do not include a title in the abstract.

The general question for this poster is: Is prenatal testosterone linked to poor school performance? The purpose of the poster is to encourage research on this topic. If a research topic cannot easily be stated as a question, it can be stated as a hypothesis (but it should be stated as a closed, falsifiable question). The hypothesis is the statement of what you expect to find. Remember, the null hypothesis is the statement of what you expect NOT to find.
