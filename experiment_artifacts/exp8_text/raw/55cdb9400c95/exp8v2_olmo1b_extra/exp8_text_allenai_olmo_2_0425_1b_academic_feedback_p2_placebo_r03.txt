Reflective thoughts

Reflective journal entries should be written in a highly personal style, as if you were thinking aloud to someone else. (See the "academic writing style" section, above, for general advice on academic writing.) Some specific advice on how to write a reflective essay follows.

Reverse outline: writing from the past

Start with a piece of writing, perhaps a journal entry or essay, that you have already written, but that you now think is really bad for one or more of the following reasons:

  • It's very long.
  • It has no structure.
  • It's riddled with mistakes.
  • It's riddled with inconsistencies.
  • It's riddled with repetitions.
  • It has no structure.
  • It's riddled with inconsistencies.
  • It's riddled with repetitions.
  • It's too wordy.
  • It's unclear, and hence not useful for future reference.
  • It's unclear, and hence not useful for future reference.
  • I don't like it.
  • I don't know what to do with it.

Go back to that writing, and try to identify problems with it. For each problem, write down the problem, and then try to come up with a solution for it.

Write a new reflection on your bad writing

Now, write down a new reflection on the previous one. Your reflection can be either longer or shorter than the bad essay. You may find it useful to simply write down the problem for each item on the list above. You can also try to come up with an improved solution for each item.

Write consistently

  • Write new reflections in an appropriate style.

  • Write each reflective entry in a coherent sequence.

  • Avoid has-been language.

  • Avoid jargon. For example, avoid "conversing" with yourself in the first person if the essay is not written in the first person.
