Software does not suffer from mechanical wear, but it decays relative to its operating environment. An update is the mechanism that reconciles that divergence.

At a baseline level, software is a series of assumptions about its underlying environment: that a specific system call behaves consistently, that an API contract remains stable, and that a particular cryptographic cipher remains computationally difficult to break. Over time, those assumptions fail. Protocols evolve, dependencies deprecate methods, and underlying operating systems change their resource-handling logic. Without updates, static code slowly drifts into functional obsolescence simply because the infrastructure beneath it refused to stay still.

More urgently, updates alter the economics of vulnerability exploitation. When a flaw is cataloged in the National Vulnerability Database (NVD) or patched upstream, the vulnerability ceases to be theoretical—it becomes documented blueprint material for automated scanners. In production environments, deferring an update does not preserve stability; it intentionally widens the window between the public disclosure of an exploit and the remediation of the attack surface.
