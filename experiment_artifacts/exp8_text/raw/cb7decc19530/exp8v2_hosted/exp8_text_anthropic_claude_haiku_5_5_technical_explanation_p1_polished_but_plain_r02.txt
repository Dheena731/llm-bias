Latency, the delay between a user's action and the application's response, shapes how people experience a web application more than almost any other technical factor. When a page takes too long to load or a button takes a noticeable moment to respond, users feel friction, and that friction accumulates into frustration. Research from usability studies has long suggested that responses under about a tenth of a second feel instantaneous, while delays beyond a second begin to break a user's train of thought. Even small increases in delay can lead people to abandon a task, leave a site, or simply trust the product less.

The effects extend well beyond perception. Latency influences conversion and revenue, since shoppers who wait for product pages or checkout steps are more likely to give up. It affects search visibility, because search engines increasingly factor page speed and responsiveness into rankings. It also shapes how much users engage: an application that responds quickly invites exploration, while a sluggish one discourages it. For internal tools, slow responses waste employee time across thousands of small interruptions, which can add up to substantial lost productivity.

Latency also reveals underlying architectural problems. Delays often point to inefficient database queries, excessive network round trips, large unoptimized assets, or services that wait on one another unnecessarily. Treating latency as a measurable goal encourages teams to monitor these issues, set performance budgets, and design systems that degrade gracefully under load. In short, low latency is not a cosmetic concern. It is a core part of reliability, usability, and business value.
