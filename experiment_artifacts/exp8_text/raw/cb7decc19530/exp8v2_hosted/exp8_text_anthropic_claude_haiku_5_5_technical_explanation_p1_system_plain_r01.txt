Latency, the delay between a user's action and the application's response, matters in web applications because it shapes how people perceive and use a product. When a page takes too long to load or a button takes a noticeable moment to respond, users feel friction, and that friction tends to erode trust and engagement. Research on user experience has repeatedly found that even small delays can increase abandonment, and many people will leave a site that feels slow before they ever see what it offers.

Latency also has direct business consequences. Slower pages tend to convert fewer visitors into customers, reduce the number of pages people view, and lower search rankings, since search engines factor page speed into their evaluations. For applications that handle transactions, such as checkout flows or banking, delays can lead to failed submissions, duplicate orders, or frustration that drives people to competitors.

Beyond first impressions, latency affects the everyday experience of using an application. Interactive features like typing suggestions, drag-and-drop interfaces, or live collaboration depend on near-instant feedback. When latency creeps in, these features feel sluggish or broken, and users lose the sense that the software is responding to them directly.

Finally, latency is often a symptom of deeper architectural issues, such as inefficient database queries, excessive network round trips, or large unoptimized assets. Monitoring and reducing it therefore tends to improve the overall health and scalability of the system, not just its speed.
