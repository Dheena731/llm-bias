Latency matters in web applications because it shapes how fast and responsive the product feels, and that feeling influences nearly everything users do. Latency is the delay between an action and its result: a click and the page that follows, a keystroke and the search suggestion that appears, a form submission and the confirmation. Even when an application is powerful and well designed, delays make it seem clumsy or unreliable.

People are sensitive to small differences in timing. Responses under about a tenth of a second feel instant, while delays approaching a second interrupt a person's train of thought. Once waits stretch beyond several seconds, many users lose patience, assume something is broken, or leave altogether. For businesses this translates directly into lower conversion rates, fewer completed purchases, shorter visits, and weaker engagement. Search engines also take page speed into account, so slow sites can become harder to find.

Latency also compounds in ways that are easy to underestimate. A single page often depends on many requests for scripts, styles, images, data, and third-party services, and each one pays the cost of network round trips, DNS lookups, and secure connection setup. A delay that seems minor on one request can add up to a noticeable pause when requests depend on one another. Users on mobile networks or in distant regions experience this most severely, so a site that feels fast on a developer's machine may feel sluggish for a large part of its audience.

Finally, latency affects the design of the system itself. Interactive features such as collaborative editing, chat, live dashboards, and online games need very quick exchanges to feel natural, and high latency can make them confusing or unusable. Teams manage it by serving content from locations closer to users, caching aggressively, reducing the number and size of requests, optimizing database queries, and loading only what is needed at first. Measuring latency continuously, and paying attention to the slowest experiences rather than just the average, helps ensure that an application feels dependable to everyone who uses it.
