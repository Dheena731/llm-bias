Thank you for sharing the draft. I wasn't given the poster itself, so this note covers the issues that most often decide whether a poster works at a conference. Please weigh each point against what you see on the page.

The strongest posters can be understood in about thirty seconds from a few feet away. Check whether your title and main finding meet that test. The title should say what you found, not just what you studied, and a single sentence near the top stating the central result or contribution will help passersby decide to stop. If a reader has to hunt through the poster to learn your conclusion, move it up and make it larger.

The second concern is density. Drafts tend to carry too much text, often because the poster is a compressed version of a paper. Try cutting the background and methods sections by half, and let figures carry the argument wherever possible. Each figure should have a short caption that states its takeaway, so a viewer does not need to decode axes and legends to learn why it matters. Make sure fonts are legible from a distance, with body text no smaller than about 24 points and section labels clearly larger.

Third, look at the flow. Viewers should be able to follow the sequence of question, approach, results, and implications without being told where to start. A clear left-to-right or top-to-bottom layout, consistent alignment, and generous white space will do more for readability than any decorative element. Keep the color palette restrained, and confirm that your charts remain readable for colorblind viewers and in grayscale.

Finally, give the conclusion section real attention. Two or three concise statements about what the work shows, what its limits are, and what comes next will give you an easy opening for conversation. Adding contact details or a way to reach the full paper is also worthwhile.
