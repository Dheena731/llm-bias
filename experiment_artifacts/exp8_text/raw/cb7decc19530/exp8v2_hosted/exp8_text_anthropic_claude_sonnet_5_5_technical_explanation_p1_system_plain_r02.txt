Latency is the delay between a user's action and the system's response, and it matters in web applications because people experience it directly as the speed and quality of the product. When someone clicks a button, submits a form, or loads a page, even small delays change how the application feels. Responses under about a hundred milliseconds seem instant, while delays of a second or more interrupt a person's train of thought, and delays of several seconds often lead them to give up entirely.

This has real business consequences. Studies from large companies have repeatedly shown that added latency reduces conversion rates, lowers engagement, and increases bounce rates. A slow checkout page costs sales, and a slow search results page makes people search less. Search engines also factor page speed into rankings, so poor latency can reduce traffic before users ever arrive.

Latency also compounds in modern architectures. A single page load may involve DNS lookups, a TLS handshake, several API calls, database queries, and requests to third-party services. Each step adds its own delay, and when requests depend on one another, the delays add up rather than overlap. A service that seems fast in isolation can feel sluggish once it sits at the end of a long chain of calls.

Physical distance sets a floor that cannot be engineered away. Data travels at a finite speed, so a user far from the server will always wait longer than one nearby, which is why content delivery networks, edge computing, and regional deployments are common. Mobile and unreliable networks make this worse, since higher and more variable round-trip times magnify every unnecessary request.

Finally, latency affects perceived reliability. Users often cannot distinguish between a slow application and a broken one, so high latency erodes trust. Teams that measure and budget for latency, paying attention to the slowest responses rather than just averages, tend to build applications that feel responsive and dependable.
