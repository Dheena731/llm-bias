Dear Alex,

Thank you for sending the draft of your literature review on remote mentoring practices in early-career research. You have clearly done substantial reading, and the review already has a sound structure: it moves from definitions to the main strands of evidence and ends by identifying a gap. The strongest section is the middle, where you compare studies of structured versus informal mentoring and draw out the tension between perceived support and measurable outcomes. That comparison is analytical rather than merely descriptive, and it gives the review its argumentative spine.

The most pressing concern is the framing in the introduction. You describe the topic as settled in one sentence and as contested in the next, and the reader is left unsure which claim you are defending. I would suggest committing to the contested position early, stating precisely what the disagreement is, and then using the rest of the review to show how the literature divides along methodological lines. At present, the reader must infer the organising question, and a clear research question stated at the end of the introduction would help considerably.

Several paragraphs summarise studies one after another, each beginning with the author's name and the year. This makes the review read like an annotated list. Where possible, group studies by claim, method, or context, and let the paragraph's topic sentence state what the group of studies shows collectively. Several studies are also cited for findings they do not quite support. The 2017 survey, for example, measured satisfaction with mentoring rather than productivity, yet it is presented as evidence about publication rates. Please recheck each citation against the claim attached to it.

The methodological critique in the penultimate section is thoughtful but underdeveloped. You note that most included studies rely on self-report and small samples, but you do not yet explain how this limitation bears on the conclusions. A sentence or two on how self-report might inflate perceived benefit, or how small samples limit generalisation to other disciplines, would make this section do real work.

Finally, check the consistency of terminology. You use "mentoring," "supervision," and "guidance" interchangeably, and readers may take these to mean different things. Either define them once and hold to the definitions, or explain why you treat them as equivalent.

Overall, this is a promising draft with a clear direction. Tightening the framing, regrouping the evidence, and verifying the citations would bring it close to submission standard. I am happy to look at a revised version.

Best wishes,
Dr. Morgan Ellis
