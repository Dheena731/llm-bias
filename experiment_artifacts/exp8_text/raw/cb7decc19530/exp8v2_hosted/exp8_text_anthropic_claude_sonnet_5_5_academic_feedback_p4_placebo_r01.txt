**Feedback on your poster draft**

**Main point:** A poster works when a passerby can grasp your central message in under a minute. Make sure your main finding or argument is stated in one sentence, near the top, in the largest body text on the poster. If a reader has to hunt for it, move it.

**What to check:**

1. **Title and takeaway.** The title should say what you studied or found, not just the general topic. Put a one-sentence conclusion where the eye lands first.
2. **Reading order.** Arrange sections (background, question, methods, results, conclusion) in clear columns or numbered blocks so no one wonders where to go next.
3. **Text load.** Cut paragraphs to short phrases or bullets. Anything you would naturally explain aloud can be left off the page.
4. **Figures.** Each figure or table should support one point, with a plain-language caption saying what the reader should notice. Make labels and axis text readable from about two meters away.
5. **Jargon.** Define any specialized term or abbreviation the first time it appears, since conference visitors may come from neighboring fields.
6. **Conclusions.** Limit them to what your results support. Briefly state limitations and next steps.
7. **Visual consistency.** Use one or two fonts, a restrained color palette, strong contrast, and enough white space.
8. **Practical details.** Include your name, affiliation, and contact information or a QR code for the paper or data, if you have one.

**Next step:** Ask a colleague outside your subfield to look at the poster for 30 seconds, then say what they think your main point is. If their answer differs from yours, revise the top of the poster first.
