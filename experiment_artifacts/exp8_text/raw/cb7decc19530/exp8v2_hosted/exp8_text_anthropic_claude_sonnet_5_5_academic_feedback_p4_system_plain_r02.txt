Thank you for sharing the draft of your poster. I haven't seen the actual file, so this note is a general sketch of the feedback I would give on a first draft. Adapt it to your poster and tell me if you'd like comments on specific sections.

The strongest posters can be understood in about a minute from several feet away. Check whether your title and main finding meet that test. The title should be readable from across the aisle, and a visitor should be able to state your central claim after a quick glance. If the main result is buried in the middle of a dense block, consider moving it to a prominent spot, perhaps a single sentence in larger type near the top or at the center.

The draft will probably benefit from less text. Posters often read like shortened papers, but visitors tend to look at figures first and read only what the figures prompt them to ask about. Trim the introduction and methods to a few short sentences each, and let your figures carry the results. Each figure should have a brief caption that states what the reader should take away, not just what is plotted. Make sure axis labels, legends, and tick marks are large enough to read at poster distance, and use consistent colors and fonts throughout.

Pay attention to the visual flow as well. A clear reading order, whether in columns or in a left-to-right sequence, helps visitors follow your argument without you there to guide them. Generous white space and aligned edges make the layout look deliberate and easier to scan. Keep the background simple and make sure the contrast is strong enough for text to stay legible under conference lighting.

Finally, end with a concise conclusions section of two or three clear statements, and add contact information or a QR code so interested people can follow up. If you can, prepare a thirty-second spoken summary to go with the poster, since it will shape how you decide what stays on the page.
