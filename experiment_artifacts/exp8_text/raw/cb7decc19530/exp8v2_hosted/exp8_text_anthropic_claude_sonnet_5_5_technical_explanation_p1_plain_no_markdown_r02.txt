Latency is the delay between a user's action and the application's response, and it shapes almost everything about how a web application feels. When a page takes a long time to load or a button takes a noticeable moment to react, people perceive the application as sluggish or unreliable, even if it is otherwise well built. Research on user behavior has repeatedly shown that small increases in delay lead to higher abandonment rates, fewer completed purchases, and lower engagement. Delays of even a few hundred milliseconds can break a person's sense of direct interaction, and delays of a few seconds often cause them to leave entirely.

Latency also compounds in ways that are easy to underestimate. A single page may require many sequential requests for markup, scripts, data, and images, and each one pays the cost of network round trips, DNS lookups, and connection setup. Because these costs are largely bound by the speed of light and the distance to the server, adding bandwidth does not always fix them. This is why techniques like caching, content delivery networks, connection reuse, and reducing the number of dependent requests are so important.

Beyond user experience, latency has practical business consequences. Search engines factor page speed into rankings, so slow sites can become harder to find. Slow responses also tie up server resources for longer, which can reduce how many users a system can handle at once. For interactive features such as chat, collaborative editing, or online games, low latency is not just a nicety but a requirement for the feature to work at all. In short, latency affects satisfaction, revenue, scalability, and the basic usability of a web application.
