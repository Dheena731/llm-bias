Latency, the delay between a user's action and the application's response, matters in web applications because people notice and respond to it, often long before they can articulate what is wrong. A page that takes a second too long to load or a button that hesitates before reacting makes an interface feel sluggish and untrustworthy, and users tend to abandon tasks or leave the site altogether. Research from various industry studies has repeatedly linked small increases in load time to measurable drops in conversion, engagement, and return visits, which is why latency is often treated as a direct business concern rather than a purely technical one.

Latency also compounds across the many requests a modern web page makes. A single page may depend on dozens of network calls to fetch scripts, styles, images, and data from servers, and each delay adds to the total wait the user experiences. Because the slowest step in a chain often determines the overall response time, even modest delays in one service can ripple outward and degrade the whole experience. Mobile users and people on weak connections feel these delays most acutely, so latency also bears on accessibility and fairness across different users.

Finally, latency shapes how software is designed. Developers reduce it through techniques such as caching, content delivery networks that place data closer to users, efficient database queries, and asynchronous loading that lets the most important content appear first. Keeping latency low is therefore not just about speed for its own sake but about making an application feel responsive, reliable, and worth using.
