Thank you for sharing this draft. It shows a solid command of the field, and the effort you have put into gathering sources is evident. My comments below are intended to help you move the review from a competent summary of existing work toward a more purposeful argument.

The most significant issue is organization. At present the review proceeds largely source by source, with each paragraph describing what a given author did and found. This makes the material easy to follow but leaves the reader to work out how the studies relate to one another. I would encourage you to restructure around themes, debates, or methodological approaches instead. Grouping studies by the questions they address, and then showing where they agree, conflict, or leave gaps, will give the review a clear line of reasoning and will make your own contribution easier to locate.

Related to this, the draft is more descriptive than critical. You report findings accurately, but you rarely evaluate them. Consider asking of each major study what its sample, design, or theoretical framing allows it to claim, and what it cannot. Where several studies share the same limitation, say so explicitly, since patterns of weakness are often what justify a new project. A few well-chosen evaluative sentences will do more for your credibility than additional citations.

The introduction and conclusion would also benefit from sharper framing. The opening currently establishes the general importance of the topic but does not state the specific scope of the review or the question guiding it. Likewise, the ending summarizes the preceding sections without clearly stating the gap your research will address. A closing paragraph that moves directly from the identified limitations to your research question would give the whole piece a stronger sense of direction.

On sources, the review leans heavily on work from the last decade, which is reasonable, but a few foundational texts are missing, and the reader may wonder how the current conversation developed. A brief historical sketch, even a paragraph, would help. I would also check that you are relying on primary studies rather than secondary descriptions of them in a few places where the citations seem to come through review articles.

At the sentence level, the prose is generally clear, though some paragraphs begin with long lists of names and dates. Leading with the idea and placing the citations in support of it will improve readability. Watch also for repeated transitional phrases such as "furthermore" and "in addition," which often signal that the connection between points has not yet been articulated. Finally, please check the reference list against the in-text citations, as I noticed a few inconsistencies in formatting and at least one missing entry.

With a reorganization around themes and a stronger critical voice, this could become a very effective review. I am happy to look at a revised outline before you redraft in full.
