Latency is the time that passes between a user's action and the application's response, and it shapes how a web application feels more than almost any other technical factor. When someone clicks a button, submits a form, or loads a page, they expect something to happen almost immediately. Delays as small as a few hundred milliseconds are noticeable, and once they stretch beyond a second or two, people begin to lose focus, doubt that their action registered, or abandon the task altogether.

This matters first for user experience. A fast application feels reliable and trustworthy, while a slow one feels broken even when it works correctly. Interactions that respond quickly encourage people to keep exploring, whereas lag interrupts their train of thought and makes simple tasks feel like chores.

It also matters for business outcomes. Research from many companies has repeatedly linked higher latency to lower conversion rates, shorter sessions, and higher bounce rates. In e-commerce, a slow checkout can cost sales directly. In productivity tools, sluggish responses reduce the time people are willing to spend using the product. Search engines also factor page speed into their rankings, so poor latency can reduce visibility as well.

Latency compounds in modern web applications because a single page view often involves many steps: DNS lookups, establishing secure connections, fetching scripts and stylesheets, calling several APIs, and querying databases. Each step adds its own delay, and sequential dependencies mean those delays accumulate. Users on mobile networks or in distant regions feel this most, since every round trip to a server costs more time on a slower or longer connection.

Because of this, teams treat latency as a design constraint rather than an afterthought. Common approaches include serving content from locations close to users, caching aggressively, reducing the number and size of requests, loading only what is needed first, and optimizing slow database queries. Measuring real-world latency, especially at the slower percentiles that the unluckiest users experience, helps reveal problems that averages hide. In the end, reducing latency is one of the most direct ways to make a web application feel better, perform better commercially, and serve a wider range of users well.
