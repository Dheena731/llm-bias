Most software updates are unglamorous: a patched library, a changed default, a fix for a crash that only happens on certain printers. They matter because software is full of known flaws, and the flaws don't stay secret for long once they're found. Security researchers, attackers, and vendors all read the same patch notes, and a fix often tells attackers exactly where the weakness was.

The 2017 Equifax breach is a clear case. The vulnerability was in Apache Struts, a web framework, and a patch had been available for about two months before attackers used it to pull personal data on roughly 147 million people. WannaCry, the ransomware that spread that May, exploited a Windows flaw that Microsoft had fixed a couple of months earlier. Many machines were still running without the patch when it hit, and hospitals and car factories were among the places disrupted.

Updates also carry smaller, less dramatic fixes: a file-sync bug that silently corrupts documents, a driver that drops Bluetooth connections, a certificate that expires and breaks logins. Skipping them usually doesn't cause an immediate failure. It just means you're carrying known problems with a growing list of people who know how to exploit them.
