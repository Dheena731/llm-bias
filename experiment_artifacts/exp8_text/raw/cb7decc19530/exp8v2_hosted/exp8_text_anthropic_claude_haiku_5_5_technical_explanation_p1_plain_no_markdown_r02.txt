Latency, the delay between a user's action and the application's response, matters because it shapes how people perceive and use a web application. Even small delays are noticeable, and when pages or interactions lag, users tend to feel that the product is slow, unreliable, or poorly built, even if everything ultimately works. Studies of user behavior have repeatedly found that longer load times lead to higher abandonment rates, meaning visitors leave before completing tasks such as signing up, searching, or making a purchase. For businesses, this translates directly into lost revenue and fewer conversions.

Latency also affects engagement and trust. Users who experience responsive interfaces are more likely to explore features, return later, and form a positive impression of the brand. Delays in feedback, such as a button that takes seconds to respond or a form that hesitates before submitting, can cause people to click repeatedly or assume the action failed, which creates confusion and extra load on the server. Search engines also consider page speed when ranking results, so slow applications can lose visibility as well as users.

Beyond the user experience, latency is a useful indicator of system health. Rising response times often signal bottlenecks in databases, network routes, or third-party services before those problems become outright failures. Keeping latency low therefore requires attention to architecture, caching, efficient code, and the physical distance between servers and users, and it remains one of the most practical measures of whether a web application is meeting the needs of the people using it.
