Latency, the delay between a user's action and the system's response, matters in web applications because it shapes how people perceive and use a site. When a page takes too long to load or a button takes a noticeable moment to respond, users feel friction, and that friction tends to translate into abandoned visits, lower engagement, and lost revenue. Research from major e-commerce and search companies has repeatedly shown that even delays of a few hundred milliseconds can measurably reduce conversions and click-through rates.

Latency also affects trust. A slow application can feel unreliable or poorly built, even when its content is good. Users often assume that if a site is sluggish, its underlying systems or its security may also be weak. Search engines have incorporated page speed into their ranking signals, so high latency can reduce visibility as well as satisfaction.

For interactive applications, the stakes are even higher. Real-time features such as chat, collaborative editing, online games, and live dashboards depend on quick round trips between client and server. Small delays can disrupt the sense of immediacy that makes these experiences work, causing lag, out-of-sync data, or frustrating interruptions.

Finally, latency compounds across a system. A page may make dozens of requests to databases, APIs, and third-party services, and each added delay stacks up. Reducing latency therefore often requires attention to the whole chain, including network distance, server processing, database queries, and front-end rendering. Teams that measure and manage latency deliberately tend to build applications that feel fast, scale more gracefully, and serve users better.
