Several claims in this essay rely on specific facts, statistics, and interpretations that come from other sources, yet very few of them are cited. The paragraph on economic trends is the clearest example: the figures about employment and growth are presented as established fact, but readers have no way to check where they came from or how current they are. The same problem appears when you paraphrase other scholars' arguments without naming them, which can make borrowed ideas look like your own and weakens your credibility.

To fix this, go through the draft and mark every sentence that depends on outside information, including numbers, historical claims, direct quotations, and any argument you did not originate. Add a citation in the required style for each one, and make sure every in-text reference matches an entry in your bibliography. Where you cannot find a source, either do the research to support the claim or soften or remove it. Proper citation will not only protect you from accusations of plagiarism but also strengthen your argument by showing that it rests on reliable evidence.
