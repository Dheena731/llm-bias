Latency compounds in ways that are easy to miss when you test on a fast office connection. A page that makes thirty API calls one after another, each taking 150 ms over a mobile network, spends 4.5 seconds doing nothing but waiting, before any rendering or JavaScript runs. The fix is often not faster servers but fewer round trips, so latency problems tend to show up as architecture problems.

Tail latency matters too. If a dashboard request fans out to forty internal services, the response is only as fast as the slowest one. A service that is fast 99% of the time will still make the page feel broken for a large share of loads, because the odds that at least one of forty calls hits its slow path are high.

Latency also shapes behavior. Users tend to perceive interactions under about 100 ms as instantaneous, and anything past a second breaks their train of thought. A search box that takes 800 ms to show suggestions gets typed into less carefully, and a checkout button that hesitates invites a second click, which can mean a duplicate order. Measured business effects are real as well: Amazon's often-cited finding that every 100 ms of added delay cost about 1% of sales has been repeated for years, and whatever the exact figure in your product, it is worth measuring rather than assuming.
