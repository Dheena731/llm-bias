Latency, the delay between a user's action and the application's response, shapes how people experience a web application more than almost any other technical factor. When a page takes too long to load or a button takes a moment too long to respond, users feel friction, and that friction changes their behavior. Research on web performance has repeatedly found that even small delays can increase abandonment, as impatient visitors leave before content appears. For businesses, this translates directly into lost sales, fewer sign-ups, and reduced engagement.

Latency also affects how users perceive quality and trust. An application that responds quickly feels reliable and well made, while one that lags can seem broken or insecure, even when its underlying functionality is sound. People tend to associate speed with competence, so slow interactions can undermine confidence in a product before users have had a chance to judge its features.

Beyond first impressions, latency compounds over the course of a session. Every interaction that waits on a server adds up, and a workflow that requires many round trips can become tedious even if each individual delay seems minor. Reducing latency therefore improves not just single moments but the overall flow of work, making applications feel smoother and more productive.

Finally, latency has consequences for search visibility and accessibility. Search engines factor page performance into rankings, and users on slow connections or older devices are disproportionately harmed by heavy, unoptimized pages. Attention to latency is, in part, attention to making an application usable for the widest possible audience.
