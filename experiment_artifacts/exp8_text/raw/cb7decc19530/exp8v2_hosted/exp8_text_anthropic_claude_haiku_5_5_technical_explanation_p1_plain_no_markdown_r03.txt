Latency, the delay between a user's action and the application's response, shapes how people experience a website or app more than almost any other factor. When a page takes too long to load or a button takes a noticeable moment to respond, users begin to feel that the product is sluggish or unreliable, even if everything eventually works correctly. Research on user behavior has repeatedly shown that even small delays can increase abandonment, as impatient visitors leave before content appears. For businesses, this translates directly into lost sales, fewer sign-ups, and reduced engagement.

Latency also affects trust and perception. A fast response signals competence and care, while a slow one can make an application feel broken or poorly built. Users tend to attribute delays to the company behind the product, so sluggish performance can damage a brand's reputation well beyond the moment of frustration.

Beyond the user's experience, latency matters for search visibility and operational efficiency. Search engines consider page speed when ranking results, so slow sites may appear lower in search listings. Latency in backend services can also compound across multiple requests, creating bottlenecks that limit how many users a system can serve at once and driving up infrastructure costs. For these reasons, reducing latency is a central concern in web design and engineering, touching everything from how code is written to where servers are located.
