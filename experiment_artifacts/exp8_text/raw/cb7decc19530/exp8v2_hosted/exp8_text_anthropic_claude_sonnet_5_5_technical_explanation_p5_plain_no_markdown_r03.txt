An API rate limit is a cap on how many requests a user, application, or device can send to an API within a set period of time, such as 100 requests per minute or 10,000 per day. Providers put these limits in place to keep their services stable and fair. Without them, a single client sending a flood of requests, whether by accident or on purpose, could slow the service down for everyone else or even bring it down entirely. Limits also help control infrastructure costs and make abuse, such as scraping or brute-force attacks, harder to carry out.

When a client goes over its limit, the API usually rejects further requests until the time window resets. The most common response is an HTTP status code 429, which means too many requests, and it is often accompanied by a header telling the client how long to wait before trying again. Many APIs also include headers that show how many requests remain in the current window, which lets developers adjust their behavior before they hit the ceiling.

Developers typically deal with rate limits by spacing out their requests, caching results they have already fetched, batching multiple operations into one call when possible, and retrying failed requests after a delay that grows with each attempt. Different plans or pricing tiers often come with different limits, so heavier users may be able to pay for a higher allowance.
