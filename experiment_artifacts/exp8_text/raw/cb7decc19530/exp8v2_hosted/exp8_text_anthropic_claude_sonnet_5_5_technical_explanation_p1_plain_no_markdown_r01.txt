Latency is the delay between a user's action and the application's response, and it shapes how a web application feels more than almost any other technical factor. When someone clicks a button, submits a form, or loads a page, they expect something to happen almost immediately. Research on human perception suggests that responses under about a tenth of a second feel instantaneous, while delays beyond a second begin to interrupt a person's train of thought. Once delays stretch to several seconds, many users lose patience and leave.

This has direct business consequences. Slow sites tend to have higher bounce rates, lower conversion rates, and weaker engagement. Even small increases in load time have been linked to measurable drops in sales and page views for large retailers and media companies. Search engines also take page speed into account when ranking results, so poor latency can reduce visibility as well as satisfaction.

Latency also compounds in modern applications. A single page may trigger dozens of requests to servers, databases, and third-party services, and each one adds its own delay. Because many of these steps depend on one another, a small slowdown in one place can ripple outward and make the whole experience sluggish. Physical distance matters too, since data can only travel so fast across networks, which is why techniques such as caching, content delivery networks, and efficient database queries are so important.

Finally, latency affects trust. Users tend to read slowness as a sign of unreliability, and they may wonder whether an action succeeded or whether the site is broken. A responsive application feels polished and dependable, while a laggy one creates frustration even when it works correctly.
