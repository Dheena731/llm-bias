Latency, the delay between a user's action and the application's response, shapes how people experience a web application more than almost any other factor. When a page takes too long to load or a button takes a noticeable moment to respond, users begin to feel that the product is sluggish or unreliable, even if everything eventually works correctly. Research on user behavior has repeatedly shown that even small delays can lead to lower engagement, higher abandonment rates, and fewer completed purchases or sign-ups. For businesses, this translates directly into lost revenue.

Latency also affects how search engines evaluate a site. Performance metrics tied to loading speed and responsiveness influence search rankings, so slow applications can become harder for new visitors to find. Beyond discovery, low latency signals that a system is well built and maintained, which builds trust.

Finally, latency compounds. A single request may seem fast on its own, but modern applications often make many calls to servers, databases, and third-party services in sequence. Small delays at each step add up, and a page that feels acceptable in testing can become frustrating under real-world conditions, such as on a weak mobile connection or during periods of heavy traffic. For this reason, teams that care about user experience treat latency as a core design concern rather than an afterthought.
