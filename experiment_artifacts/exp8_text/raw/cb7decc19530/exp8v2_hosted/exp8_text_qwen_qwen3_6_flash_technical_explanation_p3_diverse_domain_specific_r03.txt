Software updates are less about adding capability than about arresting decay. In practice, every deployed system accumulates friction: transitive dependencies shift behavior without breaking builds, heap fragmentation turns manageable allocations into paging events, upstream APIs change wire formats that silently corrupt payloads, and known exploit surfaces widen as public CVEs age out of their mitigation windows. 

A release is a controlled mutation of a live codebase. Engineers manage it by tracking version skew across clusters, verifying runtime compatibility against patched OS kernels or container runtimes, and staging rollouts behind feature flags so telemetry can prove whether an error rate actually drops instead of migrating to another endpoint. Skipping or delaying updates doesn’t preserve stability; it leaves the system operating on outdated assumptions until a routine request hits a mismatched contract and cascades. Updates matter because they keep the actual runtime aligned with the documented one.
