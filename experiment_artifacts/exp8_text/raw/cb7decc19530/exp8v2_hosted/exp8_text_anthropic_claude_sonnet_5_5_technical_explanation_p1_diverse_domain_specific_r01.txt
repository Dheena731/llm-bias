Latency matters in web apps because it compounds. A single slow request is rarely the problem. The problem is a chain of small delays that each wait on the one before.

Loading a dashboard might involve a DNS lookup, a TCP and TLS handshake, the HTML, and a JavaScript bundle that then calls `/api/me`. That response triggers three more calls. Each step costs at least one round trip. On a mobile connection with an 80 ms RTT, five dependent requests add 400 ms before the server does any work. More bandwidth doesn't help, because physics sets the floor: Sydney to a Virginia data center is roughly 200 ms round trip on any connection.

How the delay feels depends on the interaction. Under about 100 ms, a click feels like direct manipulation. Around a second, users notice they're waiting. Past that, they start double-clicking, which is how you get duplicate form submissions and double-charged orders. In an autocomplete box, a 300 ms response can arrive after the user has typed another character, so stale results flicker in and replace fresh ones.

Averages also hide the worst of it. If a page load fans out to 20 backend calls and each has a 1% chance of being slow, about 18% of page loads hit at least one slow call. The users with the largest accounts and the most data tend to feel this the most.

That is why teams track p95 and p99 rather than the mean, and why they flatten request waterfalls before tuning any single query.
