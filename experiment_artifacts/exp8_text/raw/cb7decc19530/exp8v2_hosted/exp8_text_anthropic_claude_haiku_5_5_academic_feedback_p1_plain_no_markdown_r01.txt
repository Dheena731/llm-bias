Thank you for sharing the draft of your literature review. You have a clear sense of the field's main debates, and your opening paragraph sets up the review's scope well. Your decision to organize the middle sections around methodological approaches rather than publication date is a strong choice, because it lets the reader see how the studies relate to one another.

The main concern is that the review often describes sources one after another without evaluating them. Several paragraphs move from "Smith found X" to "Jones argued Y" without explaining how these findings bear on each other or on your research question. Try to end each section with a synthesis sentence that states what the literature collectively shows, where it agrees, and where it leaves a gap. This will also make the case for your own study more persuasive.

Some claims in the third section need stronger support. Phrases such as "it is widely accepted" or "researchers have consistently shown" should either be backed by specific citations or softened. Where you draw on a single study for a broad conclusion, note its sample size, context, or design limitations so the reader can judge its weight.

Finally, the conclusion introduces a concept, the distinction between institutional and individual barriers, that does not appear earlier in the review. Either introduce it in the body where it can be developed or remove it from the conclusion. Once these points are addressed, the review should read as a coherent argument rather than an annotated list, and I would be glad to look at a revised version.
