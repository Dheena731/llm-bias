6th Circuit Judge William L. Presten

In 2016, U.S. District Judge William L. Presten ruled that Owen’s right to due process violated the First Amendment. Owen’s application was denied by the court; in a 3-2 decision, the majority held that the Bureau of Prisons had violated Owen’s rights under the First Amendment. The court’s analysis revealed a constitutional gap in the Bureau’s prison population statistics—there is no way to measure how many inmates are incarcerated and for what reason. For example, an inmate may be incarcerated for a year and serve 30 days on federal probation, and so his prison term could be 12 months in total, but the Bureau would report him as having served 11 months in prison. Presten reasoned that such inconsistent reporting amounts to a deprivation of freedom. The majority also looked at the potential threat to public safety that is implied in the federal prison system as it currently exists. If the Bureau can’t guarantee that an inmate won’t reoffend, then the inmate should not be incarcerated. But the Bureau’s statistics do not include this information, so the Bureau is mandated to incarcerate every inmate. Presten’s dissenting opinion, joined by Judge Chesney and Judge Schuler, criticized the majority for not following precedent and for facing an impossible task, along with noting that the Bureau of Prisons is at odds with other agencies within the federal government that use data from the Bureau to make decisions about the lives of the inmates. The Bureau of Prisons, meanwhile, uses data from the Bureau of Justice Statistics to make decisions about the lives of inmates.
