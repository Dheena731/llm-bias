When was it written?

Write about the time you wrote the above not the time you took to write it.

In an ideal world, let your readers know when you wrote it. Who was around, what was happening in the wider context of the time? Does it have a specific date? Was anything else happening at the same time? If you have a web address, let your readers know whether you are attaching a copy or a link. If you are following a pattern, you should explain when you are following that pattern.

What do people think?

Write about how you think, rather than how others think. Why did you think that?

If you are writing about the thoughts of others, you should give credit to them and make sure they are in the right context. Write about what they said and what context they said it in. Write about how you think about that. (Remember, it is not necessarily the same thing.)

Who should read what?

Write about the people who should read what. Your mother? Your best friends? Your boss? Your email recipient? Your potential buyer? Your doctor?

Your mother may not be interested in how you think about the risks during pregnancy, but her pregnancy may have been interesting to you. You may not be interested in the risks to your email recipient, but he may be interested in your opinions.

Do your best to keep in mind the people who are the intended readers of the piece.

Is it a letter?

If the piece is a letter, write about the audience. Who are they? Why have you written to them? What do you think they will feel if they read the letter? What is your common ground for writing to them?

If the piece is a memo, write about the office. Where are you? Who are you working for? Who are the people who work there? What is your relationship to the people who work there? What do you think they will feel when they read the memo?

Between these two models, you can explore a lot of different possibilities for writing. A memo needn't always be a business memo, and a letter doesn't always have to be a letter to a mother.

Here are some more questions to consider:

  • Do you feel the content is complete?
  • Are you satisfied with your purpose?
  • Do you really know the people you are writing to? What do you know about them?
  • What do you want to say to them? What are their needs and wants?
  • What are your assumptions about your audience and content?
  • What is your relationship with your audience?



Write a news paragraph about the same event.

Your news paragraph should have no more than four sentences. The words above, "Write a news paragraph" throw a lot of directions at you, and it can be difficult to know where to start.

Well, you don't need to know where to start. A good news paragraph is one that is short and sweet. It tells a clear, simple story.
