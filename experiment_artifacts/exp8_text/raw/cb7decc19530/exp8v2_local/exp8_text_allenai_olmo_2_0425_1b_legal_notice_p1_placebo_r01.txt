Images of commercial and public property are protected by copyright law. Not all images are sufficiently original by themselves to qualify as an independent copyright. In general, images of historic sites and buildings are typically copyrighted, and images of works of art on public property are protected by copyright law as well. Images of works of art that are owned by individuals, such as photographs of the Mona Lisa, are not copyrightable. A photograph of a work of art by someone else is only an infringing derivative work if the photograph is false, misleading, or made without permission. Infringement can occur when an image is used without permission or in a manner that misleads the audience. In most cases, the photograph is not being used in a manner that is false, misleading, or made without permission.

What should I do if I use an image that is owned by someone else?

  •  Remember that copyright exists throughout a photograph, including the background. If the photograph includes a copyrighted image in the background, then you cannot use the photograph if you used a filter or tool to create the background.
  •  If you are using an image of a work of art owned by someone else, copyright law does not prohibit you from using the image, unless you are making a derivative work of the work of art. It is good practice to obtain permission from the owner of the work of art, whether or not you will be making a derivative work.
  •  A photograph of a work of art that is in the public domain is not an infringing derivative work. The public domain means that the work is not copyrighted.

If I am on a bus or train and I put a photograph of my family on my seat, is that a copyright violation?

No. As long as you have not altered, copied, or used the photograph in a manner that is false, misleading, or made without permission, there is no copyright violation. The vehicle manufacturer owns the copyright to the seat. However, if you are using the seat to sell a product, you must seek permission to use the seat.

What is a work made for hire?

A work made for hire is a work that:

  • is created by an employee within the scope of his/her employment
  • is created by a contractor under a written contract that specifically describes the work as a work made for hire; and
  • is created by an employee or independent contractor under a work made for hire agreement, and the contractor owns the copyright to the work.

Work made for hire rules vary from state to state. In general, anything created by an independent contractor for a museum must be considered a work made for hire. However, a museum may be able to use freely a work made for hire if the work is a work in the public domain.

What is the difference between a work-for-hire and a work-made-for-hire?

The difference between a work-for-hire and a work-made-for-hire is that a work-for-hire is created by an employee within the scope of his/her employment, whereas a work-made-for-hire is a work created by a contractor under a written agreement that specifically describes the work as a work made for hire.
