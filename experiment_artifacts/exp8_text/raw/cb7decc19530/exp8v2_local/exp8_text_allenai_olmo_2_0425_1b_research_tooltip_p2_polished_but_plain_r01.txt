A simple 'confidence' interval is a range of numbers. The mean (µ) is in the middle of the interval, and the standard deviation (σ) will be different for each confidence level. For example, let's say we randomly selected data on the price of a car, and we want to estimate the population mean. We take a random sample of 50 cars, and the mean price is $25,000. We want to estimate the population mean price in the next 20% of the population, and we choose a confidence level of 95%, so we want to estimate that 20% of the population will have a price between $23,000 and $28,000. We will use this interval to estimate that the population mean price is $27,000.

The above example is an example of a simple confidence interval, and this is the type of interval that's most commonly used. These intervals are often used when you're interested in the mean of an entire population (such as all the prices of the 50 cars this example is based on), but you are also interested in the distribution of the data around this mean (such as the range of prices of all the cars, or the price of the 20% of cars with the highest prices). These intervals are also useful when you want to choose a sample size from a population that is not well understood, but you want to be able to extrapolate your sample results to the population.

More sophisticated confidence intervals are based on a hypothesis about the mean of the population, and are used to test this hypothesis. For example, let's say we want to test whether the population mean price of the cars is $27,000. We take a random sample of 50 cars, and calculate their sample mean price to be $27,000. We want to estimate the population mean of $27,000, and we want to test the null hypothesis that the population mean price is $27,000. We calculate the confidence interval for the sample mean, and our confidence interval is $26,2k to $29,9k. We reject the null hypothesis, and conclude that there is evidence that the population mean price is greater than $27,000. (Note that in practice, we can't reject the null hypothesis, we can only find evidence that it is false. We can say that the evidence is "significant", but we can't say it is "significant" enough to reject the null.)

This is because the width of a confidence interval is proportional to the sample size, and proportional to the confidence level.
