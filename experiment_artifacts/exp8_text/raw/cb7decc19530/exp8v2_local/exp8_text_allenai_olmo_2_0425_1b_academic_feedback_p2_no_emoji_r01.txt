When is a reference not a reference? 

You should not cite someone who is on the government list of ineligible candidates (who has no right to petition). An ineligible person is not in the U.S. legally. Some examples that would prevent you from citing them as an “independent” expert include: someone who is on the U.S. government list of ineligible candidates; those under arrest as of the date of the document; U.S. citizens who are being held in custody by the government; persons of unsound mind; and those applying for asylum. You should always read more carefully and always ask whether your witness can be trusted, and if the answer is “no”, you should not cite them. 

To make this point, we added a “Should I cite this?” question at the end of the document. We encourage you to answer that question. 

Who should I send my references? 

Minimum requirements

We do not have a fixed list of people you should send as references. However, we need to know at least 3 things about you from different sources:

  • Your education
  • Your employment
  • Your work experience

Your “education” means your high school diploma, GED, and undergraduate (or graduate) degrees. We need to know your educational background, because it will be a key part of how we assess your credentials.


We need to know your work experience. This includes:

  • Your last job
  • How long you worked at that job
  • Your grade point average (GPA) at that job
  • Any professional certifications you have
  • If you have any other professional credentials.
  • Contact information for your employers, including: their name, address, phone numbers, and email addresses (if relevant)

We allow you to add a “Summary of work experience” and “Education summary” at the beginning of your petition. These are your opportunities to address these items. 

Why do I need to include references? 

Legal background

We must have independent knowledge of your background to evaluate your credentials. A reference is a way for us to get both that knowledge and access to someone who can confirm the facts and information you provide.

Opinion on your qualifications

Your references will be asked to describe your professional credentials. If the reference has no opinion on whether you are qualified, the reference should say so (for example “I would trust Rusty with any project”). Your reference may describe your credentials as “excellent” or “exceptional” if they have significant experience in your field. If they have not worked in your field, the reference should say so (for example “I am not a professional in this field, but Rusty has worked as an engineer in a variety of engineering jobs”). 

In general, you should only use references on a USCIS form if you have a professional credential (for example, a Bachelors degree) that your references can describe. If your reference is not a professional with credentials, this should be noted on the form with a statement such as “I am not a professional in this field, but Rusty has worked as an engineer in a variety of engineering jobs.
