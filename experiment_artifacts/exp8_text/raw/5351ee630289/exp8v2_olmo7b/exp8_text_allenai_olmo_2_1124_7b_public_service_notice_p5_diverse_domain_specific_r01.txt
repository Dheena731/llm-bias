"Make the writing sound natural". "Make the writing sound conversational"; "Make the writing sound like it's from a native speaker". These are all reasonable criteria, and the TLP is intended to test them. However, one has to be careful not to confound fluency in a language with understanding of the language. The TLP contains many examples of the linguistic production of people with normal fluency who display all the hallmarks of near-native speech. Yet these are often the same people who cannot understand jokes or metaphors or apparent contradictions.

Fluency is about producing the language, not understanding it. This is one of the reasons that we adopted a more naturalistic approach to the TLP than is normal: we are moving away from thinking about language as something that is generated by a single processor, and more towards thinking about language as something that emerges from a set of interacting agents. The language that we see is not the one that is modelled in the brain, but rather the result of a process whereby different sub-systems cooperate and compete.

The TLP is intended to test this process. The questions in the TLP are of two types: those that can be answered by simple rule-based procedures, and those that cannot. The former are things like grammatical gender or the form of a verb, and this is where people with alexia are likely to have the most difficulty. The latter are things like jokes and metaphors and apparent contradictions and this is where people with fluency are likely to have the most difficulty. The former is a problem of coding (the TLP may have too many such examples, but then again it may not), the latter is a problem of decoding. It is not clear how many people with fluency will be able to answer the more demanding questions, but those who can will be able to distinguish their understanding of language from that of people with alexia.

In short, the TLP is intended to test the ability to process language, not the ability to generate it. Fluency and near-native fluency arise from the ability to process language, and so is the ability to answer questions about language that cannot be answered by simple rule-based procedures. This is what we mean by "understanding".

I would like to see the TLP being used in a similar way to how the APG test is used in the Netherlands. Here people are given a short sentence (like "The boy kicked the ball") and are asked to identify which word is the subject of the sentence. The sentence is flashed up on a screen for half a second, and the subject can be identified by pressing a key. The test is scored on a scale of 0 to 4, with 0 indicating no response and 4 indicating correct identification.

If we think about what this test is testing, we can see that it is not testing the ability to read or to write or to speak or even to understand. It is testing the ability to identify the subject of a sentence, and the fact that this can be done so quickly and accurately is evidence that this is a skill that we all have.

If we can identify the subject of a sentence, then we can identify the verb of that sentence, and we can identify the direct object of the sentence, and we can identify the indirect object of the sentence. We can identify the preposition, and the adjective, and the adverb. We can identify the noun, and the pronoun, and the adverbial expression, and the conjunction, and the determiner, and the interrogative word, and the particle, and the phrase.
